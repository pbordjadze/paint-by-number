Guardian: Dino. Keep only this, described in words: one plesiosaur with its long neck curled back into an S, its small head turned sideways to look straight at the viewer with one narrow, sly, knowing eye, four paddle flippers spread, a plump body. Nothing else of any modern drawing: no blue-green and cream colouring, no modern anatomy, no smooth modern rendering.

Step 2, after the Crystal Palace dinosaurs of Benjamin Waterhouse Hawkins, 1854: a Victorian life-size sculpture as an object in a museum photograph. Cast cement ("artificial stone") over a brick and iron core, painted in dull Victorian colours: dark slate grey and brownish olive, with a dirty ochre underside; the paint weathered and patched with honest repairs. The 1850s reconstruction: a very long, stiff, swan-like neck, a lizard- or crocodile-like head with a row of teeth, a thick lizard body, scaly hide modelled with a tool in the cement, paddles like a turtle's. The neck still curls back into an S and the head turns to give that sly sideways eye. It sits on a rough cement plinth. Not a toy, not resin, not a figurine: a big heavy public sculpture.
Step 3, after Duria Antiquior, 1830: a small lithograph print lying flat, the way 1830 lithographs were made: drawn on stone with greasy lithographic crayon, so the tones are a soft grainy black-grey with crayon texture, fine hatching and a printed border line; then coloured by hand with thin transparent watercolour washes (a muddy blue-grey sea, a brownish creature, a pale sky), a little outside the lines. The 1830 idea of the animal: a long snaky neck, a small lizard-like head, a turtle-like body with paddles; the neck curled into an S, the head turned back with the sly eye. Some sea around it. Wove paper slightly toned at the edges, not a modern watercolour.
