Guardian: The Baba Yaga.
Essence (always keep): the wink in old magic: wild, clever, a little dangerous, kind to the brave; a dwelling or tool that works by itself. One Baba Yaga, one hut..
May echo, only where this tradition has its own native form for it, free in shape, scale and pose: a dwelling that stands on legs (chicken legs, tree roots); the mortar and the broom as self-working tools.
Leave behind (by default): the red-kerchief roof and the woman's face in the window; the orange cartoon chicken feet with black claws; today's thick outline and saturated red, green and yellow print look; the broom leaning on the door; any pointed hat, wand or owl.

Step 1, after Ivan Bilibin (St Petersburg, 1899-1900 (Vasilisa the Beautiful) and 1911). Medium: Pen-and-ink outline tinted with watercolour, reproduced in a printed booklet with an ornamental border. What this tradition natively makes: Bilibin's Baba Yaga: a fine, even black pen outline filled with flat watercolour tints, a wide ornamental border of Old Russian pattern, flat planes with no perspective; she flies in her mortar with pestle and broom, and her forest hut stands on its legs behind a skull-topped fence (in his Vasilisa plates). Carved log walls and patterned boards; humour in the sharp-nosed face, never a Halloween witch.
Step 2, the lubok (Russia, late 17th-18th century). Medium: Woodcut from a linden-wood block, printed in black ink on paper, with a few rough flat washes of colour. What this tradition natively makes: A real 18th-century Russian lubok, the famous one where Baba Yaga rides a pig, pestle in hand, to fight the crocodile: a woodcut cut in a linden block, printed in black on coarse paper, with rough flat washes of red, yellow and green brushed on by hand, a few lines of Church Slavonic-style lettering in the block. Her, not the hut.
Step 3, after the Finno-Ugric stump storehouse (undated northern-forest tradition). Medium: Small timber storehouse raised on an upturned tree stump with its roots as legs; weathered pine logs; as shown in an open-air museum (Skansen). What this tradition natively makes: A small grey log storehouse standing on a single upturned stump whose roots splay like claws, with a cramped door high up, no window and no stove: the dwelling on legs, with no face and no magic. Photographed as the real object among moss and snow; this tradition made the idea of the hut on legs, not the picture.
