Guardian: George.
Essence (always keep): the meaning in a movement: see the music, hear the dance. One small clear note made visible as a single turn, timed to a pulse, with nothing wasted. George is one tuning fork..
May echo, only where this tradition has its own native form for it, free in shape, scale and pose: a thin looping line (a turn, a track on the floor); a beat kept (a count, a tune).
Leave behind (by default): the tuning fork and its two prongs; the en pointe pose; the blue-and-gold Ballets Russes gouache look; the small face.

Step 1, after Petipa's Imperial Ballet (St Petersburg, 1880s-1890s). Medium: Pencil on a small sheet of paper: a ballet master's working notes. What this tradition natively makes: A ballet master's pocket working sheet like Petipa's notes for the cat's dance in The Sleeping Beauty: soft pencil on small, folded, smudged paper, hasty ground-plans of the stage with dancers as small dots and crosses in lines, diagonals and one curling path, arrows for their movement, bar counts scribbled beside them, a few words (kept illegible), no colour. The dancers are marks; the pattern on the floor and the count are the dance. (The exact contents of the Bakhrushin sheet are UNCONFIRMED; this follows Petipa's known 'notes and sketches'.)
Step 2, after Christian Johansson's class (St Petersburg, 1860s-1903). Medium: Carved and varnished wood: a small violin and a thick stick, a dancing master's tools. What this tradition natively makes: A dancing master's class kit of the 1880s: a small violin of amber-brown varnished maple and spruce with a short bow, worn pale at the shoulder and fingerboard, and beside it a thick plain wooden stick for striking the time, laid on bare worn pine floorboards under cool window light. No ornament. The room is a pulse; the instrument and stick are all the music and all the instruction. (Whether Johansson's violin was a pocket 'kit' type is UNCONFIRMED.)
Step 3, after Beauchamp-Feuillet dance notation (Paris, 1700-1701). Medium: Copper engraving printed in black on laid paper: a page of dance notation. What this tradition natively makes: A page of Feuillet's Choregraphie: copper-engraved in black on cream laid paper, the dance drawn as a thin looping track down the page like a ribbon laid on the floor, with tiny step-signs like hooked and flagged notes along it, bar lines crossing the track, and the tune engraved on a staff above; plate-mark and a ruled border visible. A turn is a curl in the line; the music and the movement share one page, nothing wasted.
