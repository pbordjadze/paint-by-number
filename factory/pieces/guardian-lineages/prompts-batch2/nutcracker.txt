Guardian: The Nutcracker.
Essence (always keep): the heart in the machine: a mechanism that moves with care, as if it means well. One gentle figure..
May echo, only where this tradition has its own native form for it, free in shape, scale and pose: a figure made to move by a visible mechanism (key, cams, lever); a livery that tells its calling: uniform, habit or costume.
Leave behind (by default): the green cap and red-banded jacket; the saluting pose; the brass wind-up key as a shape; the Erzgebirge turned-toy outline and its red cheeks; the soldier as the only possible form.

Step 1, after Hoffmann's Nutcracker and the Mouse King (Berlin, 1816). Medium: Hand-coloured copperplate title plate after Hoffmann's own pen drawing, in a letterpress children's book. What this tradition natively makes: A Berlin Biedermeier children's book of 1816: a small copperplate print hand-coloured in thin transparent red, blue, yellow and green, from Hoffmann's quick, slightly caricatured pen drawing; stiff toy-people with large heads in toy uniforms, on cream paper above a letterpress caption. The Nutcracker is a toy among toys on a Christmas-eve table, drawn with affection. The surviving plate shows the Mouse King, so its exact look for a Nutcracker is inferred.
Step 2, after the Jaquet-Droz Musician (Neuchatel, 1768-1774). Medium: Clockwork automaton: brass and steel mechanism inside a costumed figure, a real organ under her fingers (materials per typical description; UNCONFIRMED against the museum record). What this tradition natively makes: The Musician as she stands in Neuchatel: a young woman seated at a small organ in a silk gown, her painted face serene, her eyes following her fingers, her chest rising as if breathing, the clockwork hidden under the dress. Her makers made a machine gentle: show care in the tilt of the head and hands, no gear visible, soft candlelight, and no real person's likeness.
Step 3, after the clockwork Monk (South Germany or Spain, c. 1560). Medium: Wood and iron with a key-wound spring mechanism, in a cloth habit; 15 inches high. What this tradition natively makes: A 16th-century monk automaton: a small friar with a hand-carved wooden face and hands in a plain habit, a rosary and a little wooden cross in his hands, caught mid-step with head bowed and eyes lifted, the iron mechanism hidden under the robe except a winding key. The look is tactile and worn: carved wood, rubbed paint, coarse cloth; the gesture of care is the subject.
