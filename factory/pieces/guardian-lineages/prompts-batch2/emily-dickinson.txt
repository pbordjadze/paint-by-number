Step 1, after Isaac Watts, as New England stitched him (New England, c. 1740-1840). Medium: Silk thread on linen: a New England schoolgirl's sampler carrying a verse of Watts. What this tradition natively makes: A New England girl's sampler: fine cross-stitch in silk on undyed linen, a few rows of alphabet, one short verse in stitched capitals (kept tiny and not legible), a border of strawberries or carnations, and a few small naturalistic motifs: a bird, a sheep, a flower spray, perhaps a bee. Threads faded to rose, ochre, sage and indigo, the linen gone cream. A domestic object, small, patient and exact: the verse is the whole point, the flower stitched flat.
Step 2, after Sternhold and Hopkins's metrical psalter (London, 1562; this form from the 1584 edition). Medium: Letterpress on paper: a small metrical psalter with printed tunes. What this tradition natively makes: An Elizabethan metrical psalter (John Day, London): a small thick book in black-letter and roman type on soft paper gone brown, each psalm a block of four-line stanzas in common metre, a short tune printed in notes on a staff above it, a woodcut-ornamented capital and a plain ruled border. A psalm is a tune and four lines, nothing more; the bee or the flower, if any, a tiny printer's ornament.
Step 3, after the medieval Psalter (England, c. 1320-1340 (as in the Luttrell Psalter)). Medium: Ink and pigment on vellum: an illuminated psalter leaf. What this tradition natively makes: A 14th-century English psalter leaf: ruled vellum with a few lines of Latin in black textura, a red-and-blue pen-flourished initial touched with gold, and in the wide margin a thin vine whose branches carry tiny quick-tinted creatures and flowers (a bee, a bird, a flower head) drawn in a few lines of ink and wash. The psalm sits small in a large margin of bare vellum; the small margin creature is as alive as the text.
